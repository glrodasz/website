> My career has been shaped by curiosity, self-learning, and the habit of building things before I felt fully ready. I started by modifying Blogger templates as a teenager, then moved through university, Java, full-stack JavaScript, developer communities, agencies, startups, and international teams. Across all those stages, the pattern has been the same: I learn by experimenting, sharing, and building with others.

## Early Experiments with Tech at School

Everything started when I was 14 and wanted to create a website, even though I had no idea how to build one. During that process, I discovered [Blogger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blogger_%28service%29) and quickly noticed that its themes had a kind of template configuration. I realized the code contained links to the images used in the blog's design, so I began replacing those links with my own images.

Eventually, I started modifying the [CSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSS), although I did not know at the time that it was CSS. I was simply experimenting, breaking things, and learning by observing what changed.

Over the next two years, I taught myself some [Visual Basic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_Basic) and [Flash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macromedia_Flash). I was also introduced to [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%2B%2B) and [Visual Basic for Applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_Basic_for_Applications) at school.

One moment I still remember clearly was during my final school project. I needed to repeat a task many times, but I had no idea that a `for` statement existed. A friend's brother-in-law explained it to me, and it felt mind-blowing. It was one of the first moments when I understood that programming was not just about writing instructions, but about finding better ways to solve problems.

That experience shaped one of the habits that has stayed with me throughout my career: learning by experimenting, observing what changes, and improving through curiosity.

## Fortunate Foundations at University

I enrolled in Systems Engineering at university mostly because my friends were doing it. At the time, I believed I could learn programming on my own through the internet, which was not entirely wrong. I was also considering a more design-oriented career, since I was exploring [Photoshop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_Photoshop) retouching and video editing with [Sony Vegas Pro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegas_Pro).

University taught me many important fundamentals, but it was during an extra summer course that I truly became interested in web development. In that program, I learned [PHP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PHP), [MySQL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MySQL), [jQuery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JQuery), and more CSS.

Although I struggled with foundational subjects like Calculus, Geometry, and Physics, that also pushed me to take more advanced courses. One of the most important was [Design Patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_design_pattern), which became crucial for my professional growth. Looking back, I am glad I took those advanced courses early, because they helped me build a stronger foundation as a software engineer.

This stage taught me that formal education and self-learning do not compete with each other; they can complement each other when curiosity is the driving force.

## First Steps into the Tech Industry

While I was still at university, I received two job offers and interviewed for both. One was for a PHP position, where I had more experience thanks to the summer course. However, the role required working with [CakePHP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CakePHP), a framework I did not particularly enjoy.

The other opportunity involved [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29) and many advanced concepts I was not familiar with yet. The person hiring me was willing to teach me, so I chose that role because I saw it as a better opportunity to learn and grow.

Unfortunately, after six months, the company went bankrupt. Thanks to a recommendation from my former boss, I joined another company in a similar role. That experience helped me grow significantly and become a more well-rounded engineer.

Looking back, that decision became an early example of choosing growth over comfort, a pattern that has repeated throughout my career.

## From Java to Full-stack JavaScript

As I continued learning, I realized that building large web applications required me to understand both Java and JavaScript. Over time, however, I found Java too verbose and complicated for the kind of work I wanted to do, while JavaScript felt more flexible and enjoyable.

Around that time, I discovered [Node.js](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Node.js), which made it possible to use JavaScript on the server. That changed the way I thought about full-stack development. Focusing on one language across both frontend and backend felt like a practical path toward becoming a stronger full-stack developer.

Like PHP, JavaScript with frameworks such as [Express.js](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Express.js) made it simple to set up a server quickly. When ES6, or [ECMAScript 2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECMAScript_version_history#6th_Edition_%E2%80%93_ECMAScript_2015), was released, I dove into its new features, which made JavaScript even more powerful and enjoyable to work with.

Frameworks like [Meteor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteor_%28web_framework%29) reinforced this idea by making it easier to build web and mobile applications with a shared codebase. This approach felt efficient, practical, and aligned with the kind of engineer I wanted to become. It solidified my decision to specialize in JavaScript.

This transition taught me to look for tools that help me move across the whole product, from interface to server, from idea to implementation.

## Transitioning to Working in English

My first job that required English was an important turning point. During the interview, the CEO told me my English level was a 3 out of 5. Surprisingly, I felt good about that, because I had expected it to be lower.

At first, I used English mostly during [stand-up meetings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand-up_meeting). I practiced basic phrases, learned how to say ticket numbers, and answered the usual questions: what I had done yesterday, what I was working on that day, and whether I had any blockers. In the beginning, I rarely gave much detail beyond mentioning the ticket numbers I was working on.

Over time, I became more comfortable, especially during technical meetings where frontend engineers discussed best practices and proposed solutions. Those discussions had to happen in English because, although most of us spoke Spanish, one engineer only spoke English. That pushed me to improve.

I also started taking English lessons, but the biggest progress came when I worked with a team where half of the people were based in New York. From that point on, English became a natural part of my work. It has remained a continuous learning process ever since.

That experience taught me that communication improves the same way technical skills do: by using it every day, making mistakes, and staying exposed to situations that force you to improve.

## Growing Developer Communities

My journey into tech communities began when I started attending local meetups such as [MedellinJS](https://www.meetup.com/medellinjs/), [MedellinPHP](https://www.meetup.com/medellin-php/), and other technology groups. I was inspired by how communities could help developers grow, share knowledge, and find opportunities.

I noticed that many community talks were valuable, but there was also room to improve how technical content was presented. That motivated me to start giving talks myself. Not because I thought I was better, but because I saw an opportunity to help others learn while deepening my own understanding.

I also encouraged others to share what they knew, emphasizing that everyone starts somewhere.

Later, I noticed there was no dedicated space for CSS enthusiasts in Medellín. Together with two friends, I created a CSS community that grew beyond our expectations. Our meetups consistently attracted around 100 attendees, which introduced new challenges, such as finding venues large enough to host everyone.

The success of that community eventually led us to organize Colombia's first CSS conference. It was a milestone that showed the growing interest in web technologies in the region.

Running these communities taught me valuable lessons about leadership, event organization, communication, and the importance of creating spaces where developers can learn from each other.

Those communities also taught me that sharing knowledge is not separate from learning; it is one of the strongest ways to deepen it.

## Building My Own Design Agency

During this period, I also started a design agency with a designer I had met at **Komet Sales**. We called it **Space Monkeys**. Together, we worked on several projects, including **Docmeti**, **Mr. Och8**, and **Speed**.

At Komet Sales, I met a designer who was unusually open to suggestions from a development perspective. At the time, the concept of a web designer was still emerging, and many designers were still working from a traditional print-design mindset. Concepts like [responsive design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responsive_web_design), [Bootstrap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bootstrap_%28front-end_framework%29), and [accessibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_accessibility) were often ignored.

With this designer, however, there was a strong understanding of how important those elements were for the web. We collaborated well because we shared the same interest in combining design quality with technical feasibility.

Eventually, he left the company, but we stayed in touch and began collaborating on small freelance projects. After a few successful projects, we saw a larger opportunity in the market. Many agencies were not fully embracing new technologies and web trends, so we decided to create our own agency with the goal of doing things differently.

That was how Space Monkeys was born.

We delivered many successful projects, but the digital agency space was highly saturated. In the end, success often depended on having access to the right clients. Larger clients were sometimes hesitant to trust a small agency, even when we produced strong results. Ironically, some larger agencies that lacked technical expertise outsourced work to us.

We worked on projects for companies such as [Argos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grupo_Argos) and **GPM**, as well as smaller projects like **La Piel Plus** and Speed. Our work ranged from e-commerce sites and mobile applications to interactive games and advanced web applications.

Running Space Monkeys was a valuable experience. I learned how to work with clients, manage employees, and understand what it takes to run a business. I also gained a deeper appreciation for design by being involved in day-to-day creative processes. It taught me how to optimize workflows, communicate better across disciplines, and build products with both technical and visual quality in mind.

Space Monkeys helped me understand that building good digital products requires more than code: it requires design awareness, communication, trust, and the ability to turn unclear ideas into working products.

## A Journey into Startups

My first real startup experience was with **Docmeti**, a personalized skincare subscription built around each customer's skin type. It was one of my first opportunities to build not only software, but also a product deeply connected to operations, laboratories, packaging, and distribution.

I worked on the platform, e-commerce flows, payments, orders, and integrations, while also contributing to the product and brand. From that project, we also developed **Mr. Och8**, a tattoo aftercare line that later helped us win **Capital Semilla** in Antioquia. It was a formative experience that taught me how much range, adaptability, and resilience startup life requires.

After that came **Workia**, a startup built around the idea of a global coworking pass. We moved quickly, launched early, solved problems in real time, and built both web and mobile experiences as the product evolved.

The company gained traction, partnerships, and investor interest, but the pandemic forced us to pivot quickly. Although the new product showed promise, the team was already worn down, and we eventually decided to close the company.

Together, those experiences gave me a broader view of what it means to build under uncertainty. Docmeti taught me the complexity of physical products and operations, while Workia showed me the speed, ambition, and emotional intensity of startup life.

Those startup experiences reinforced one of the most important lessons in my career: building products requires adaptability, resilience, and the ability to keep learning while the context around you changes.

## Embracing Remote Work

While living in Medellín, I was working on a project for a bank. As is often the case with large organizations, things moved slowly, with plenty of red tape, processes, and dependencies. There were times when I finished all my coding work within the first few hours of the day, but since I was expected to spend the full day at the office, I couldn't simply leave.

Instead of wasting that time, I started investing in my own development by reading engineering books, watching tutorials, and taking courses. One of those books was [*Remote*](https://basecamp.com/books/remote), written by the founders of [Basecamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/37signals). Until then, I thought working remotely simply meant working from home, but the book opened my eyes to a different philosophy: having more control over my time, greater flexibility, and the freedom to focus on meaningful work. That resonated with me, especially because I enjoyed building side projects and exploring ideas beyond my regular job.

I decided to look for a fully remote opportunity and remembered meeting someone from [Auth0](https://auth0.com/) at JS Conf Argentina. I already knew the product and its reputation in the tech industry, so I applied through their website. The interview process was particularly interesting: instead of a traditional technical test, I had to build a small project while pretending I was already part of the team. They evaluated not only my code but also how I communicated, asked questions, and handled uncertainty. Eventually, I got the job and became Auth0's second employee in Colombia.

Joining Auth0 completely changed my professional landscape. I was suddenly working with talented people from around the world, which pushed me to raise my standards as an engineer. I also returned to full-stack JavaScript development, working with Node.js in production, and discovered new areas of interest such as [identity management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_management) and [OAuth 2.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OAuth#OAuth_2.0). Working remotely also accelerated my English learning, since around 90% of my daily communication happened in English.

Beyond the technical experience, Auth0 introduced me to company offsites, which I have come to appreciate at every company since. They showed me how important it is to build genuine relationships with colleagues, even in fully remote environments. I also had the opportunity to travel to Seattle and Buenos Aires, and learned to collaborate across time zones with teammates in Europe.

Looking back, joining Auth0 was much more than getting a remote job. It changed how I understood work, professional growth, and collaboration, opening the door to a global career.

## A New Chapter: Moving to Sweden

In early 2019, a friend who had moved to Sweden about six months earlier asked if I was interested in applying to [Klarna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klarna), the company that had relocated him there. True to my habit of staying open to new opportunities, I asked my partner what they thought about moving. The answer was: "Let's pack our bags."

The process was demanding, and I tried not to get my hopes up until it was over. Even so, I really enjoyed it. Although Klarna was technically a bank, the whole process felt more like working with a digital agency.

The final round of interviews took place in Stockholm, with all expenses covered. On the longest leg of the trip, from Bogotá to Frankfurt, I was lucky enough to be upgraded to first class. It was my first time in Europe, and the most comfortable flight of my life. The interviews went very well, and I received an offer.

However, I had two big fears. The first was financial: in Colombia, I was well established at Auth0, earning in US dollars and spending in pesos. The salary in Sweden was somewhat better, but not a dramatic change, and the country is known for its high taxes. On top of that, living in Colombia was much cheaper than earning and spending in a local European currency.

The second fear was going back to working in an office. I had been working remotely for almost three years, I had really enjoyed it, and I wasn't sure how hard it would be to adjust again.

In the end, I took the risk, and it turned out to be one of the best decisions of my life. It taught me that some of the best opportunities come when you are willing to step out of your comfort zone, even when the numbers and the comfort of the familiar point the other way.

## Exploring Live Streaming in Twitch

In early 2020, when the pandemic began, the Swedish government recommended working from home. I was spending my days in my first apartment in Sweden, and I felt a growing urge to create more content and more courses, beyond the ones I had already recorded on platforms like [Platzi](https://platzi.com/). I kept asking myself why I should announce that I'm going to record a course, disappear, and only show up when it's finished.

At the same time, I had a symmetrical 500 Mbps connection at home, something that would have been unthinkable in Latin America back then. A balanced connection like that was extremely hard to find in a residential setting. It felt like the perfect opportunity to try [live streaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_streaming) on [Twitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twitch_(service)) and share the process of creating a course with the world, including how I prepare and how I study.

So I started a series of streams where we built a real project, with all the foundations needed to reach production. We called it "from zero to production", with the idea of reusing that same project in the final course. That addresses one of the biggest challenges in course creation. Depending on the level you aim for, you either need a project that truly reaches that level, or you fall back on small, simple examples.

It was a period of a lot of learning and networking. Thanks to streaming, I met incredible people and companies, and it opened doors to talks and other communities. The community we built around the streams also led us to start thinking about creating a bootcamp, which I'll talk about later.

It taught me what it means to be on social media and to create content from a different perspective, exposed in front of an audience. I also learned a lot on the technical side, from hardware and good audio to video editing and formats. And it showed me the reality of streaming. I miss it a lot and I had a great time, but it consumes a lot of time. While I could, I made the most of it and practiced one of my favorite things, teaching.

Teaching is the best way to learn for me. It always has been, and I think it always will be.

## Creating CSS Conf Colombia

From 2018 to 2023, I ran the **Medellín CSS** community together with two other organizers, Estefan Aguilar and Luisa Vaca. We organized more than 20 events, mostly meetups with two talks each, along with workshops for the community. Compared with the well-established communities in Medellín at the time, ours was remarkably successful. We averaged around 100 people in the room, to the point that finding a venue for that many people became a real challenge.

Thanks to that experience, we decided to embark on a bigger adventure and organize an international conference. Estefan had already given a talk at CSS Conf, so we spoke with its organizer and received permission to create the Colombian edition. From that moment, it took us a year of preparation. By then I had already planned my move to Sweden, so I had to organize most things from there.

I can't thank Henry Sarasa enough. He was another of the organizers and, in practice, my eyes, my feet, and my body in Medellín. Even though I had many contacts from past ventures, I had nobody on the ground to take care of all those errands, and Henry was always attentive and ready to help.

Unfortunately, the conference was scheduled for 2020. A week before the event, with the speakers' tickets, the hotel, the venue, the equipment rental, and the internet all arranged, and with my own flight booked for that weekend, we learned that the venue was cancelling every event. What made it worse was that the venue didn't have the professionalism to warn us in advance. We found out through social media, and had to deal with a storm of questions and decisions.

Fortunately, we handled it well. We talked to our sponsors and agreed to postpone. Nobody knew how the pandemic would evolve, and anyone who lived through it knows it took far more than a year or two to return to normal. So we refunded the tickets, increased the number of speakers, and turned it into a fully online conference.

It was a beautiful event, and an incredible experience even though it had to be virtual. To this day, when I run into the speakers, they thank me for it. It was a surprise for them too, and they remember it as a very well-done piece of work.

It is also very hard work, and I would think twice before doing it again. Still, it taught me that a community can take on a much bigger challenge than anyone expects, and that the care you put into the people who join you is remembered long after the event ends.
