> I've always learned by experimenting, and I've often started building things before feeling fully ready. I began by modifying Blogger templates as a teenager, and since then I've gone through university, Java, full-stack JavaScript, developer communities, agencies, startups, and international teams. The pattern has been the same all along. I learn by trying things and building with other people.

## Early Experiments with Tech at School

It all started when I was 14 and wanted to create a website, even though I had no idea how to build one. That's when I found [Blogger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blogger_%28service%29) and noticed that its themes had a kind of template configuration. The code had links to the images used in the blog's design, so I started replacing them with my own images.

After a while I started changing the [CSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSS) too, although I didn't know it was CSS at the time. I was just experimenting, breaking things, and learning from what changed.

Over the next two years I taught myself some [Visual Basic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_Basic) and [Flash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macromedia_Flash). At school I was also introduced to [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%2B%2B) and [Visual Basic for Applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_Basic_for_Applications).

One moment I still remember clearly was during my final school project. I had to repeat a task many times and I had no idea a `for` statement existed. A friend's brother-in-law explained it to me and it blew my mind. That's when I started to understand that programming isn't only about writing instructions. It's about finding better ways to solve problems.

## Fortunate Foundations at University

I enrolled in Systems Engineering mostly because my friends were doing it. I thought I could learn programming on my own through the internet, which wasn't entirely wrong. I was also considering a more design-oriented career, because I was exploring [Photoshop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_Photoshop) retouching and video editing with [Sony Vegas Pro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegas_Pro).

University taught me many fundamentals, but it was in an extra summer course that I really got interested in web development. There I learned [PHP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PHP), [MySQL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MySQL), [jQuery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JQuery), and more CSS.

I struggled with subjects like Calculus, Geometry, and Physics, and that pushed me to take more advanced courses. One of the most important was [Design Patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_design_pattern), which turned out to be crucial for my professional growth. I'm glad I took those courses early, because they gave me a stronger foundation as a software engineer.

## First Steps into the Tech Industry

While I was still at university, I got two job offers and interviewed for both. One was a PHP position, where I had more experience thanks to the summer course. But it required working with [CakePHP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CakePHP), a framework I didn't particularly enjoy.

The other involved [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29) and many advanced concepts I didn't know yet. The person hiring me was willing to teach me, so I chose that one. It looked like a better chance to learn and grow.

Unfortunately, the company went bankrupt after six months. Thanks to a recommendation from my former boss, I joined another company in a similar role. I grew a lot there and became a more well-rounded engineer.

## From Java to Full-stack JavaScript

As I kept learning, I realized that building large web applications meant understanding both Java and JavaScript. Over time I found Java too verbose and complicated for the kind of work I wanted to do, while JavaScript felt more flexible and more fun.

Around then I discovered [Node.js](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Node.js), which let me use JavaScript on the server. That changed how I thought about full-stack development. Using one language on both the frontend and the backend felt like a practical way to become a stronger full-stack developer.

Like PHP, JavaScript with frameworks such as [Express.js](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Express.js) made it easy to set up a server quickly. When ES6, or [ECMAScript 2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECMAScript_version_history#6th_Edition_%E2%80%93_ECMAScript_2015), came out, I dove into the new features, and they made JavaScript even more powerful and enjoyable.

Frameworks like [Meteor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteor_%28web_framework%29) pushed me further in that direction by making it easier to build web and mobile apps from a shared codebase. It felt efficient, practical, and close to the kind of engineer I wanted to be. So I decided to specialize in JavaScript.

## Transitioning to Working in English

My first job that required English was an important turning point. In the interview, the CEO told me my English was a 3 out of 5. Surprisingly, I felt good about that, because I expected it to be lower.

At first I only used English in [stand-up meetings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand-up_meeting). I practiced a few basic phrases, learned how to say ticket numbers, and answered the usual questions about what I did yesterday, what I was working on that day, and whether I had any blockers. In the beginning I rarely said more than the numbers of the tickets I was working on.

Over time I got more comfortable, especially in technical meetings where the frontend engineers discussed best practices and proposed solutions. Those discussions had to be in English because one engineer only spoke English, even though the rest of us spoke Spanish. That pushed me to improve.

I also started taking English lessons, but the biggest progress came when I worked with a team where half of the people were in New York. From then on, English became a natural part of my work, and I'm still learning it.

Communication improves the same way technical skills do. You use it every day, you make mistakes, and you stay in situations that force you to get better.

## Growing Developer Communities

I got into tech communities by attending local meetups such as [MedellinJS](https://www.meetup.com/medellinjs/), [MedellinPHP](https://www.meetup.com/medellin-php/), and other technology groups. I liked how they helped developers grow, share knowledge, and find opportunities.

I noticed that many talks were valuable, but there was also room to improve how the technical content was presented. That motivated me to start giving talks myself. Not because I thought I was better, but because I saw a chance to help others learn.

I also encouraged others to share what they knew, since everyone starts somewhere.

Later, I noticed there was no dedicated space for CSS enthusiasts in Medellín. Together with two other organizers, Estefany Aguilar and Luisa Vaca, I created **Medellín CSS**, a community that grew beyond our expectations. Between 2018 and 2023, we organized more than 20 events, mostly meetups built around a main talk, sometimes with more, along with workshops for the community. Compared with the well-established communities in Medellín at the time, ours was remarkably successful. Our meetups consistently attracted around 100 attendees, which introduced new challenges, such as finding venues large enough to host everyone.

The success of that community eventually led us to organize Colombia's first CSS conference. It was a milestone that showed the growing interest in web technologies in the region.

## Building My Own Design Agency

During this period, I also started a design agency with a designer I had met at **Komet Sales**, someone unusually open to suggestions from a development perspective.

Back then the concept of a web designer was still emerging, and many designers were still working with a traditional print-design mindset. Things like [responsive design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responsive_web_design), [Bootstrap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bootstrap_%28front-end_framework%29), and [accessibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_accessibility) were often ignored.

With him it was different. He understood how important those things were for the web, and we shared the same interest in combining design quality with technical feasibility. We collaborated well.

Eventually he left the company, but we stayed in touch and started collaborating on small freelance projects. After a few successful ones we saw a bigger opportunity. Many agencies weren't fully embracing new technologies and web trends, so we decided to create our own agency and do things differently.

That's how **Space Monkeys** was born.

We delivered many successful projects, but the digital agency space was very saturated, and success often depended on having access to the right clients. Larger clients sometimes hesitated to trust a small agency, even when we produced strong results. Ironically, some larger agencies that lacked technical expertise outsourced work to us.

We worked on projects for companies such as [Argos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grupo_Argos) and **GPM**, and on smaller ones like **La Piel Plus** and **Speed**, along with **Docmeti** and **Mr. Och8**. The work ranged from e-commerce sites and mobile apps to interactive games and advanced web applications.

Running Space Monkeys, I learned how to work with clients, manage employees, and what it takes to run a business. Being involved in the creative process every day also gave me a deeper appreciation for design, and I got better at optimizing workflows, communicating across disciplines, and building products with both technical and visual quality in mind.

## A Journey into Startups

My first real startup experience was with **Docmeti**, a personalized skincare subscription built around each customer's skin type. It was one of my first chances to build not just software but a product tied to operations, laboratories, packaging, and distribution.

I worked on the platform, e-commerce flows, payments, orders, and integrations, and I also contributed to the product and the brand. From that project we developed **Mr. Och8**, a tattoo aftercare line that later helped us win **Capital Semilla** in Antioquia. It taught me how much range, adaptability, and resilience startup life asks for.

After that came **Workia**, a startup built around the idea of a global coworking pass. We moved fast, launched early, solved problems as they appeared, and built web and mobile experiences while the product evolved.

The company gained traction, partnerships, and investor interest, but the pandemic forced us to pivot quickly. The new product showed promise, but the team was already worn out and we eventually decided to close the company.

Docmeti taught me how complex physical products and operations are, and Workia showed me the speed, ambition, and emotional intensity of a startup.

## Embracing Remote Work

While I was living in Medellín, I was working on a project for a bank. As often happens in large organizations, things moved slowly, with a lot of red tape, processes, and dependencies. Sometimes I finished all my coding in the first few hours of the day, but I was expected to spend the full day at the office, so I couldn't just leave.

Instead of wasting that time, I started investing in myself by reading engineering books, watching tutorials, and taking courses. One of those books was [*Remote*](https://basecamp.com/books/remote), written by the founders of [Basecamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/37signals). Until then I thought working remotely just meant working from home, but the book showed me a different philosophy, with more control over my time, more flexibility, and the freedom to focus on meaningful work. That resonated with me, especially because I enjoyed building side projects and exploring ideas beyond my job.

I decided to look for a fully remote job and remembered meeting someone from [Auth0](https://auth0.com/) at JS Conf Argentina. I already knew the product and its reputation, so I applied through their website. The interview process was interesting. Instead of a traditional technical test, I had to build a small project while pretending I was already part of the team. They evaluated not only my code but also how I communicated, asked questions, and handled uncertainty. I got the job and became Auth0's second employee in Colombia.

Auth0 completely changed my professional landscape. I was suddenly working with talented people from around the world, and that pushed me to raise my standards as an engineer. I also went back to full-stack JavaScript, using Node.js in production, and found new areas of interest like [identity management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_management) and [OAuth 2.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OAuth#OAuth_2.0). Working remotely also sped up my English, since around 90% of my daily communication was in English.

Beyond the technical side, Auth0 introduced me to company offsites, which I've come to appreciate at every company since. They showed me how important it is to build real relationships with colleagues, even when everyone is remote. I also got to travel to Seattle and Buenos Aires, and I learned to collaborate across time zones with teammates in Europe.

Auth0 was much more than a remote job. It changed how I understand work, professional growth, and collaboration, and it opened the door to a global career.

## A New Chapter: Moving to Sweden

In early 2019, a friend who had moved to Sweden about six months earlier asked if I was interested in applying to [Klarna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klarna), the company that had relocated him there. True to my habit of staying open to new opportunities, I asked my partner what they thought about moving. The answer was "Let's pack our bags."

The process was demanding, and I tried not to get my hopes up until it was over. Even so, I really enjoyed it. Although Klarna was technically a bank, the whole process felt more like working with a digital agency.

The final round of interviews took place in Stockholm, with all expenses covered. On the longest leg of the trip, from Bogotá to Frankfurt, I was lucky enough to be upgraded to first class. It was my first time in Europe, and the most comfortable flight of my life. The interviews went very well, and I received an offer.

However, I had two big fears. The first was financial. In Colombia I was well established at Auth0, earning in US dollars and spending in pesos. The salary in Sweden was somewhat better, but not a dramatic change, and the country is known for its high taxes. On top of that, living in Colombia was much cheaper than earning and spending in a local European currency.

The second fear was going back to working in an office. I had been working remotely for almost three years, I had really enjoyed it, and I wasn't sure how hard it would be to adjust again.

In the end, I took the risk, and it turned out to be one of the best decisions of my life. It taught me that some of the best opportunities come when you step out of your comfort zone, even when the numbers and the familiar point the other way.

## Exploring Live Streaming in Twitch

In early 2020, when the pandemic began, the Swedish government recommended working from home. I was spending my days in my first apartment in Sweden, and I felt a growing urge to create more content and more courses, beyond the ones I had already recorded on platforms like [Platzi](https://platzi.com/). I kept asking myself why I should announce that I'm going to record a course, disappear, and only show up when it's finished.

At the same time, I had a symmetrical 500 Mbps connection at home, something that would have been unthinkable in Latin America back then. A balanced connection like that was extremely hard to find in a residential setting. It felt like the perfect opportunity to try [live streaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_streaming) on [Twitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twitch_(service)) and share the process of creating a course with the world, including how I prepare and how I study.

So I started a series of streams where we built a real project, with all the foundations needed to reach production. We called it "from zero to production", with the idea of reusing that same project in the final course. That addresses one of the biggest challenges in course creation. Depending on the level you aim for, you either need a project that truly reaches that level, or you fall back on small, simple examples.

It was a period of a lot of learning and networking. Thanks to streaming, I met incredible people and companies, and it opened doors to talks and other communities. The community we built around the streams also led us to start thinking about creating a bootcamp, which I'll talk about later.

It taught me what it means to be on social media and to create content from a different perspective, exposed in front of an audience. I also learned a lot on the technical side, from hardware and good audio to video editing and formats. And it showed me the reality of streaming. I miss it a lot and I had a great time, but it consumes a lot of time. While I could, I made the most of it and practiced one of my favorite things, teaching.

Teaching is the best way to learn for me. It always has been, since my first talks at the meetups, and I think it always will be.

## Creating CSS Conf Colombia

When we decided to take on an international conference, our biggest advantage was the experience we had gained running the meetup community. Estefany had already given a talk at CSS Conf, so we spoke with its organizer and received permission to create the Colombian edition. From that moment, it took us a year of preparation. By then I had already planned my move to Sweden, so I had to organize most things from there.

I can't thank Henry Zarza enough. He was another of the organizers and, in practice, my Avatar in Medellín. Even though I had many contacts from past ventures, I had nobody on the ground to take care of all those errands, and Henry was always attentive and ready to help.

The conference was scheduled for 2020, yes, that year many of us buried at the back of our memories. A week before the event, with the speakers' tickets, the hotel, the venue, the equipment rental, and the services all arranged, and with my own flight booked for that weekend, we learned that the venue was cancelling every event. What made it worse was that the venue didn't have the professionalism to warn us in advance. We found out through social media, and had to deal with a storm of questions and decisions.

We managed to handle it well. We talked to our sponsors and agreed to postpone. Nobody knew how the pandemic would evolve, and anyone who lived through it knows that getting back to normal took much more than anyone could have imagined. So we refunded the tickets, increased the number of speakers, and turned it into a fully online conference in 2021.

It was a beautiful event, and an incredible experience even though it had to be virtual. To this day, when I run into the speakers, they thank me for it. It was a surprise for them too, and they remember it as a very well-done piece of work.

Although it was a wonderful challenge, I would think twice before doing it again. That said, it taught me that a community can take on a much bigger challenge than anyone expects, and that the care you put into the people who join you creates much more impact than you can imagine.
